# Copywriting Craft

This is a LIVING skill. It is seeded from the method in Ann Handley's "Everybody Writes" (Wiley,
2014), distilled and rewritten as our own practitioner notes with attribution, and it is meant to
grow: add house examples, before/afters from our own sites, and new tells as we find them. Do not
treat the current text as final; extend it.

Why it exists right now: our marketing copy has read as obviously AI-generated. Section 3 is the
direct fix. The rest is the underlying craft that makes the fix stick.

## How this fits with the other skills

- **product-positioning** decides WHAT to say: the value hierarchy, the hero message, outcome over
  features. Do that first. This skill assumes the message is chosen and makes the writing good.
- **landing-page-revamp** owns page architecture (section order, trust surface). This skill owns the
  sentences inside those sections.
- **content-engine** owns content strategy and the production pipeline at scale. Writer agents in
  that pipeline should load THIS skill for the writing itself.
- **seo-aeo-geo** owns discoverability mechanics. Its sourcing discipline (claims need sources)
  applies to copy too; see section 7 here.

## 0. The quality bar

A piece of copy earns publication only if it is useful to the reader, has something fresh in it,
and is written from the reader's side of the table. Handley frames quality as the product (not the
sum) of utility, inspiration, and empathy: if any factor is zero, the piece is worth zero. A
beautifully written page that helps nobody is a zero. A useful page written with no care is a zero.
Hold every draft to all three.

Two supporting beliefs, both from the book, both load-bearing:

1. **Writing is a habit, not a talent.** Good writers are not better first-drafters; they are
   better editors of their own work. Quality lives in the rewrite. Never ship a first draft.
2. **Publishing is a privilege.** Nobody has to read this. The reader owes us nothing, and every
   sentence that wastes their time spends trust we do not have.

### Two rules we hold above the rest (Brian's standing directive)

Apply these to everything, and hardest to the copy that says what a product does (home pages, product
pages, feature explanations). They catch most AI-marketing copy on their own.

1. **Lead with the point (the pyramid principle).** State the conclusion first, then support it. The
   reader should get the answer in the opening line and be able to stop there; the reasons and detail
   pyramid underneath. Order every piece, every section, and every sentence answer-first. Never bury
   the lead, and never make the reader assemble the point from clues. (This is Barbara Minto's pyramid:
   top-line the message, stack the support beneath it. It is the same instinct as "front-load
   everything" in section 2, named and elevated because it is the one we break most.)
2. **Say it plainly (5th-8th grade).** Use short, common words a smart person in a hurry reads without
   slowing down. No jargon, no insider terms, no marketing voice, no writerly flourishes. If a
   twelve-year-old would not use the word, find a plainer one. Plain is not dumb; plain is respect for
   the reader's time.

Worked example from our own product tour (both rules at once):
- Buried and jargon-y: "Every number computed from your file" / "The figure in the sentence is
  calculated in code from your rows, never guessed."
- Lead-first and plain: **"The numbers are real, not guessed"** / "MySheetAI adds up your own data to
  get every number. It never makes one up."

## 1. Process: blank page to strong copy

Condensed from Handley's writing-GPS into the steps we actually use. Order is flexible except that
rewriting always follows drafting, never replaces it.

1. **Goal.** One line: what should this piece make the reader do or believe, and why does the
   business care? Write it at the top of the working file and leave it there.
2. **Reframe for the reader (the so-what chain).** Take the goal and ask "so what?" and answer
   "because..." repeatedly until you hit something the reader actually feels. That bottom answer
   is the message. If the chain dies at "because our product has X", you have a feature, not a
   message; go back to product-positioning.
3. **Gather the specifics first.** Concrete details are the raw material of good copy: numbers,
   named examples, a real customer's phrasing, the exact before/after. Writing generic copy is
   usually a research failure, not a writing failure. If you have no specifics, stop and get some.
4. **Outline.** A list of points in order, each one sentence. Great copy is assembled more than
   composed. Pick a deliberate shape (how-to, contrarian take, case story, comparison, Q&A) rather
   than defaulting to essay mush.
5. **Write to one real person.** Picture a specific reader you like and write the draft as if to
   them, in the second person. Not "users can", but "you can". This single move kills most
   corporate voice on its own.
6. **The ugly first draft.** Write badly, fast, with the door closed. Placeholders like
   "[better example here]" are legal. The draft's only job is to exist.
7. **Walk away.** Hours if possible, minutes if not. Editing your own words needs distance.
8. **Rewrite.** This is the real writing. Two passes, chainsaw then scalpel; see section 5.
9. **Headline last, and seriously.** Spend real time on it; it is the most-read sentence on the
   page. See section 6.
10. **Someone else edits.** Another set of eyes before it ships, always. For anything
    conversion-critical, that reviewer reads as the customer, not as a proofreader.
11. **Readability pass.** Does it LOOK inviting? Short paragraphs, subheads, bullets, white space.
    Dense slabs of text signal "work" and lose the scanner.
12. **End with a next step.** Never leave the reader standing there. One clear action, phrased as
    the value of acting, not the mechanics of it ("End scheduling hassles", not "Submit").

Deadlines are part of the craft: set one and treat it as real. Endless polishing is a form of not
shipping. Done is better than perfect.

## 2. The durable craft rules

The rules that survive contact with any format. Each one is checkable on a draft.

1. **Front-load everything.** The most important idea goes at the start of the piece, the
   paragraph, and the sentence. Kill throat-clearing openers ("It is important to note that...",
   "In today's world...", "The purpose of this page is..."). Subject and verb early; qualifiers
   later or never.
2. **Delete the running start.** Almost every draft warms up for a paragraph before it starts.
   After drafting, try lopping off the first paragraph (or the first line of an email, or the
   setup sentence of microcopy). If the meaning survives, the cut stands. It almost always survives.
3. **Show, do not tell.** Do not assert qualities ("powerful", "seamless", "effortless");
   demonstrate them with a concrete fact, a number, a scenario, or a named example. Ask what
   specific pain the product removes and put THAT pain on the page in the reader's own terms.
4. **Give things the dignity of their names.** "Geranium" beats "flower"; "your Sunday-night
   invoice pile" beats "administrative burden". Specific enough to be believable, universal enough
   to be relevant. Specificity is also the single strongest anti-generic (and anti-AI-tell) move.
5. **Make every sentence earn its keep.** Each sentence adds something the previous one did not.
   Each paragraph holds one idea. If a sentence restates its neighbor, cut it. Less, but better.
6. **Use real words.** Words you would say aloud to a person you respect. No Frankenwords, no
   business weblish ("leverage", "utilize", "bandwidth", "synergies", "solution" as a noun for the
   product), no words with bolted-on suffixes. Jargon is allowed only when the audience genuinely
   speaks it.
7. **Strong verbs, active voice, almost no adverbs.** "Production surged" beats "production
   increased quickly". Passive voice reads as evasion. An adverb is usually a confession that the
   verb was weak. Keep an adverb only when removing it changes the meaning.
8. **Analogy over abstraction.** Explain the unfamiliar with the familiar, with a twist of
   surprise. Convert big numbers into human scale. Never reach for the stock comparison (football
   fields); a worn analogy is a cliche and reads as filler.
9. **Write like a teacher, not a preacher.** Explain why, not just what. And limit moralizing:
   copy built from "never", "always remember", and "don't forget" reads as condescension.
10. **One reader per piece.** If two audiences need different messages, write two pieces. Blended
    copy serves nobody (this pairs with product-positioning's audience-split rule).
11. **Rhythm is information.** Vary sentence length. Short sentences land points. Fragments too,
    sparingly. A one-sentence paragraph is a spotlight; use it for the line that matters.
12. **Voice is a differentiator; tone flexes.** See section 4.

## 3. Copy that does not read as AI (the reason this skill exists)

AI-flavored copy is not wrong, it is HOLLOW: grammatically perfect, rhythmically monotonous,
confidently vague, and interchangeable with any competitor's page. Handley was describing lazy
human marketing copy a decade before LLMs, and the diagnosis is identical: it reads as if nobody
in particular wrote it, for nobody in particular, about nothing in particular. The cure is the
same too. Everything in this section is a specific application of "be specific, show don't tell,
write to one person, earn every sentence."

### The three tests, run on every customer-facing draft

1. **The strip-the-logo test.** Cover the product name and logo. Could this copy describe a
   hundred other companies? If yes, it says nothing; rewrite it around what is true only of us.
   (Handley: tell the story only you can tell.)
2. **The read-aloud test.** Read it out loud to an imagined specific customer. Every sentence you
   would be embarrassed to SAY to a person's face ("Unlock seamless productivity!") gets cut or
   rewritten into something you would actually say.
3. **The one-fact test.** Every paragraph must contain at least one thing that is checkably true
   and specific to us: a number, a named mechanism, a real constraint, a concrete scenario. A
   paragraph of pure adjectives and vibes is filler by definition.

### The tells, and the fix for each

| Tell | Example smell | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adjective stacking | "powerful, seamless, intuitive platform" | Replace every quality-claim adjective with the fact that would prove it. If no fact exists, the claim goes. |
| Hollow verb-object pairs | "unlock potential", "elevate your workflow", "empower teams", "streamline operations" | Say what actually happens, to whom, in plain verbs: "your invoices go out the night the job closes". |
| The triad reflex | "Fast. Simple. Secure." / "no X, no Y, no Z" everywhere | Triads are fine once. As a house rhythm they scream template. Break the pattern; make one point well. |
| "Whether you're A, B, or C..." | audience-flattering enumeration | Pick the one real reader (section 1, step 5) and write to them. |
| "It's not just X, it's Y" / "isn't just about X" | fake-profundity pivot | State Y directly. The pivot construction adds nothing but syllables. |
| Throat-clearing openers | "In today's fast-paced digital landscape..." | Delete the running start (rule 2). Start where the reader's problem starts. |
| Company-centric voice | "We offer...", "Our platform provides..." | Count "you" vs "we" on the page. "You" must win decisively. Recast sentences from the reader's side: not "we offer fast deployment" but "deploy before lunch". |
| Symmetric everything | every sentence 15-20 words, every section three bullets, every heading gerund-parallel | Vary length and structure on purpose. Perfect uniformity is a machine signature. |
| Confident vagueness | "helps teams work better together" | Vague + confident = filler. Either get specific or get honest ("early, works best for teams under 20"). Honesty is itself differentiating. |
| Unearned superlatives | "best-in-class", "industry-leading", "revolutionary" | Data before declaration (section 7). No evidence, no claim. |
| Fake empathy openers | "We know how challenging X can be..." | Empathy is demonstrated, not announced: name the exact recognizable moment of pain instead ("the 11pm CSV cleanup before the board meeting"). |
| Generic CTAs | "Get Started", "Learn More", "Submit" | Amplify the value of the act, not the act: "See your first report", "Stop chasing invoices". |
| No opinion anywhere | balanced, hedged, agreeable throughout | Real writers have a point of view and defend it. Take a side, state why, acknowledge the tradeoff (biased and balanced). Hedge-words ("can help", "may enable") get cut. |
| Em-dash confetti | dashes stitching every clause | Banned outright, house style. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses. Usually the sentence wanted to be two sentences. |

### The deeper fix

The tells are symptoms. The disease is writing without material. AI (and a rushed human) pattern-
matches its way to plausible sentences when it has no specifics to work with. So the durable
anti-AI discipline is step 3 of the process: gather real specifics BEFORE writing, then let the
copy be built out of them. A page written from five true facts about our product and one real
customer moment cannot read generic, because no one else could have written it.

When editing existing AI-flavored copy, do not polish sentence by sentence. Extract the two or
three true claims buried in it, throw the rest away, gather the missing specifics, and rewrite
from the outline up.

## 4. Voice and tone

- **Voice** is the brand's personality in words. Define it as three or four adjectives with
  contrasts ("plain-spoken, dry, confident; never wacky, never breathless") and keep it constant
  everywhere: hero, docs, error states, emails. Voice is a moat; almost nobody invests in one.
- **Tone** flexes with the reader's state while voice holds. Playful on the marketing page; calm
  and purely helpful on the error page and the billing-failure email. A joke in a failure state
  reads as mockery. Ask: what is the reader feeling right now?
- **The test**: strip the branding from a page. Would a regular reader know it is ours? That is
  voice working. (Start collecting our own voice definitions per product here as we write them.)
- Microcopy carries voice further than heroes do: 404s, empty states, confirmations, and loading
  states are read at moments of full attention. Write them deliberately: what happened, what it
  means for the reader, what to do next, in voice, in as few words as will do it.

## 5. Editing: two passes and the checklist

Never edit in one undifferentiated "polish" pass. Two passes, in order:

**Pass 1, chainsaw (structure).** Ignore the words.
- Is the key idea stated near the top? (Or is it buried under setup? Move it up, cut the setup.)
- Does anything not serve the goal line from step 1? Cut it, even if it is good.
- Does every paragraph hold exactly one idea the others do not? Merge or split.
- Is the order right? Does each point hand off to the next? Transitions should carry an idea
  forward, not just glue ("however", "moreover" are glue; an echoed idea is a transition).

**Pass 2, scalpel (words).** Now the sentences.
- Compress phrase-bloat: "in order to" becomes "to"; "despite the fact that" becomes "although";
  "the majority of" becomes "most"; "there will be times when" becomes "sometimes".
- Cut the self-evident: "in this post...", "we believe that...", "it's worth noting that...".
- Swap weak verb + adverb for one strong verb. Convert passive to active.
- Kill cliches and buzzwords on sight; replace each with the plain claim underneath it.
- Fix modifier placement ("publish only good content", not "only publish good content").
- Read it aloud once, whole. Cadence problems are audible before they are visible.

**Ship checklist** (all boxes or it does not ship):
- [ ] Goal line existed before drafting; the piece still serves it
- [ ] So-what chain reached a reader-felt outcome; that outcome leads the piece
- [ ] Written to one specific reader; "you" beats "we" on the page
- [ ] Strip-the-logo test passed (could not describe a competitor)
- [ ] One-fact test passed (every paragraph has a checkable specific)
- [ ] Read-aloud test passed (nothing you would not say to a person)
- [ ] Running start deleted; first sentence earns its position
- [ ] No tell from the section-3 table survives
- [ ] Every claim is true and supportable; superlatives have evidence or are gone
- [ ] Rewritten at least once after a real break; second person edited it
- [ ] Looks readable: short paragraphs, subheads, white space
- [ ] Ends with one clear next step phrased as value
- [ ] No em-dashes anywhere

## 6. Format quick-references

Positioning content for these lives in product-positioning and landing-page-revamp; these are the
craft notes per format.

- **Headlines.** Spend as much time on the headline as the body; it does most of the work. Promise
  specifically what the piece delivers, and deliver it (a headline is a contract; clickbait is
  breach). Put the reader in it ("...you can run on a Sunday afternoon"). Specific numbers and
  concrete nouns beat lively adjectives. Never promise "surprising" unless it surprises.
- **Home page / hero.** The job is "we get you", in the reader's words, instantly. Lead with what
  we do FOR them, not what we are. Radically simple above the fold; resist explaining everything.
  Use the vocabulary the customer uses, not our internal nouns. Three or four clear paths out,
  each framed around a reader problem. Trust signals close, they do not open.
- **Landing pages.** One page, one job. Match the message the click promised, verbatim theme, or
  the visitor feels baited. Benefit-led headline, scannable proof, minimal form fields, no
  competing navigation, and a CTA that names the value of clicking. Less is more here more than
  anywhere: every added element bleeds conversion.
- **Feature descriptions.** Feature named in the reader's terms, then the so-what chain's bottom
  answer, then one concrete proof (number, scenario, screenshot caption). Three sentences is
  usually enough. The pattern to avoid: capability, capability, adjective.
- **Email.** Write an email you would open. Short subject, front-loaded; body from a human, in
  first person to second person, one topic, one CTA. Context is the multiplier: the same email is
  filler on Monday and perfect the morning the reader hits the exact problem it solves. Respect
  the inbox: opt-in only, and prune the unresponsive rather than shouting louder.
- **About page.** It is not about us; it is about us in relation to the reader. Tell the founding
  story only we can tell, show real humans, and keep the customer the hero. This is the natural
  home of the "bigger story": the change we are trying to make in the world, told plainly.
- **Blog posts.** Earn the lede: open by putting the reader (or someone just like them) into a
  scene, a sharp question, a surprising fact, or a stage-setting story. Finish strong: recast the
  takeaway or land a closing line; do not trail off. Structure visibly (subheads, lists). Useful
  in 300 words beats padded to 1,500.
- **Error states and empty states.** See section 4. Plain statement of what happened, what it
  means, what to do next. Voice yes, jokes almost never. An empty state is an onboarding surface:
  say what will appear here and give the one action that fills it.

## 7. Truth and trust (the publishing rules)

Copy is publishing, and publishing borrows journalism's ground rules. Brand copy is trusted LESS
than journalism by default, so the bar is higher, not lower.

- **Data before declaration.** An opinion needs a reason on the page; a claim needs a source. This
  is the same gate as seo-aeo-geo's sourcing discipline: an unsourced claim is worse than no claim.
- **Cite the primary source**, not the blog that quoted it, and cite as you write; retrofitting
  attribution is how accidental plagiarism happens.
- **Fact-check the small stuff**: names, numbers, links. Sloppy details poison trust in the big
  claims.
- **Tell the whole truth.** Acknowledge tradeoffs and alternatives; "biased and balanced" (take a
  side, argue it fairly) is credible where one-sided puffery is not. Never trash competitors.
- **Only real news is news.** Internal milestones the reader would not care about do not go in the
  changelog-blog. The filter: would a real customer find this useful to know?
- **Permission, not forgiveness**, for anything we did not create: quotes beyond a snippet,
  images, data. Under 15 words with attribution where the phrasing is the point; otherwise
  paraphrase and credit.
